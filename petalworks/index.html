The Story

About the Project

Inspiration

Endless short-video feeds have trained many of us to switch every few seconds. Focus timers try to help, but most either punish you (get distracted and your plant dies) or are forgettable (a number counting down). On a headset there's one more problem: people can't wear one for hours, and an app that treats taking it off as failure doesn't fit the device.

So I made Petalworks work both ways. Your focus counts whether the headset stays on or comes off. Plant a seed on your real desk, then work wherever your task is: in VR or mixed reality, or with the headset off at your desk. When you're done, a flower is blooming where you left it. Nothing ever dies.

What it does

  • Plant a seed on your real desk and set your time, by touch or with a glance and a pinch.
  • Focus in the headset or out of it. Work in VR or mixed reality, or take the headset off for work at your desk. Your session keeps going either way.
  • Come back and watch your flower bloom, then say honestly how it went. Nothing ever dies, and honesty is never punished.
  • Your garden remembers. Over the week, your desk becomes a picture of where your focus went, and the blooms you love become part of your space.
  • Reasons to return: new flowers, small visitors and rewards that can only be earned by focusing, never bought.

No account, no ads, no data leaves the headset.

How I built it

Built in Unity with the Meta XR SDK for Quest 3 and 3S:

  • Hands and gaze through the Interaction SDK, so every action works by touch or look-and-pinch, with no controllers.
  • Scene understanding and spatial anchors, so the garden finds your desk and stays there from one day to the next.
  • Sessions that work whether the headset stays on or comes off, so switching between headset and desk never costs you your progress.
  • Tested in the Meta XR Simulator and on a real headset every build, with the UI designed for glasses-class fields of view.

What I learned

Over 20+ builds in 18 days, I logged 180+ pieces of playtest feedback, each with a fix. Three lessons shaped the app most:

  • Don't make people choose what they don't understand. A setting that confused testers became a single reassuring line.
  • Show less, sooner. New users reach their first bloom quickly, and the rest of the garden opens up as they grow.
  • A timer alone doesn't bring people back. Playtests shaped what makes the garden worth returning to, week after week.

Challenges

  • Small targets in hand tracking: forgiving targets and clear feedback, so nothing gets lost or misread.
  • Rooms I've never seen: placement and recovery that work on any desk, scanned or not.
  • Solo in about three weeks: a strict cut line, and every change checked on the headset.

What's next

Launching mid-December 2026 on the Meta Horizon Store, in time for New Year's resolutions. Free to start, with an optional one-time unlock. After launch: more to grow, more ways to interact, and more languages.

In the Headset

Hand Interactions

Touch It, or Look and Pinch

Petalworks is hands-only, end to end; a controller is never needed. It's built on the Meta Interaction SDK, and every action works two ways: by direct touch, and by looking at it and pinching. That second path keeps small objects easy to use and lets you stay seated, without reaching across the desk.

Plant

Pick up a seed and drop it in. Targets are forgiving, so a missed drop is never lost.

Set the time

Turn a real-feeling dial with your hand. Clear feedback tells you when your time is set.

Press

Buttons you press with a fingertip, with a hint that shows what each one does before you press it.

Arrange

Pick up your garden and set it down anywhere on your desk. Keep the blooms you love close by.

Play

Small visitors react when you reach out to them, by touch or by look-and-pinch.

Designed from playtests

Every gesture was tuned across 20+ headset builds and 180+ pieces of playtest feedback: first-run guidance that shows instead of tells, menus that never open by accident, and a few SDK edge cases that only showed up on a real headset.

Why VR

Technology That Gives Attention Back

I've been working in VR for the last seven or eight years, and what excites me most right now is that it's finally starting to fit around our lives instead of asking us to leave them.

For most of that time the pitch was escape: put on a headset and go somewhere else. Mixed reality flips that. Your real desk, your real room, become the canvas, and hands and eyes make the interaction feel like touching things rather than operating software. Building Petalworks, the moment that sold me was a playtester's reaction to a little bee landing on a flower on their own desk: "Loved the bee, it's too cute!!!" Nobody had to explain it.

I'm also excited about the shift toward lighter devices and shorter sessions. The future I want isn't wearing a headset for eight hours; it's a calm place you check in with for a few minutes, that remembers where you left things, and that sends you back to your day a little better off. Persistent anchors, glasses-class hardware and designing for a narrower field of view all point that way.

After years of watching the medium grow, and at a time when so much tech is built to keep us scrolling, VR has a real chance to be the opposite: technology that gives people their attention back. That's what I want to keep building.
